How to Choose Between Job Offers

TL;DR — When you have two or three offers and a deadline, the instinct is to compare base salaries and pick the biggest number. But comp is the criterion that changes least over the next two years, while manager quality, growth, and stability determine whether you're still happy in month six. This guide lists the criteria that actually predict how a job works out, the biases that push candidates toward the wrong offer, and a structured way to weigh and rank 2-3 offers in about ten minutes.

Why Offer Decisions Go Wrong

An offer decision is made under three conditions that reliably distort judgment: a deadline (often artificial and pressured), incomplete information (you spent maybe six hours with each company), and a single salient number (total comp) that dominates a dozen fuzzier factors. The result is that candidates over-weight what's easy to compare and under-weight what actually drives day-to-day experience.

The factors people regret ignoring, six months in, are almost always the same: a manager who turned out to be absent or hostile, a role that was scoped differently than the interview implied, a company whose runway or trajectory was worse than the recruiter suggested, or a commute and schedule that looked fine on paper and grinds in practice. None of those show up if you only compare offer letters.

The Criteria That Actually Matter

1. Total compensation — modeled honestly

Not base salary. Base + realistic bonus + equity value (discounted heavily for private companies) + benefits delta (health premium, 401k match, PTO) + cost-of-living adjustment if the roles are in different cities. Two offers with the same base can differ 30% on real take-home once all of this is in.

2. Career growth & learning

Will you be the most or least skilled person on the team? Is there a clear path to the next level, and people who've walked it? Does the role stretch you into skills that compound, or is it more of the same? A job that makes you more valuable in three years can be worth a large pay cut today.

3. Manager & team

The single largest driver of job satisfaction and of whether you leave within a year. You interviewed with this person — trust the signal. Did they listen, answer directly, seem respected by their reports? Ask to talk to someone on the team without the manager present. A great company with a bad manager is a bad job.

4. Work-life balance & flexibility

Real hours, not stated hours. On-call load. Remote / hybrid / in-office and how strictly it's enforced. Meeting culture. Travel expectations. A 50-hour role at 20% higher pay is a pay cut per hour, and the extra hours come out of the parts of life that don't have a salary.

5. Company stability & runway

Public and profitable is one risk profile. Series B burning cash with 14 months of runway is another. Neither is disqualifying, but they should be scored differently, and the equity in the risky one needs a bigger discount. Ask directly about runway, revenue trajectory, and last round.

6. Equity upside

Separate line item from base comp because the uncertainty is so different. For public stock, it's close to cash. For private, ask for the strike price, the last preferred price, the total shares outstanding, and the vesting schedule — then assume a wide range of outcomes including zero. Don't let a big theoretical number anchor the whole decision.

7. Location, commute & visa / relocation

A daily 45-minute commute is 375 hours a year. Relocation uproots your social network and may reset your partner's career. Visa sponsorship or lack of it can override everything. If two offers are otherwise close, this is often the tiebreaker that matters most in practice.

How to Actually Decide — Step by Step

  1. List the real options. Usually 2-3 offers, plus "stay in current job" if that's live. Include the status quo — it's a real alternative with real value.
  2. Model total comp properly for each: base + expected bonus + discounted equity + benefits + COL adjustment. Write the number down so it stops being a vibe.
  3. Pick 5-6 criteria from the list above that matter for your life stage. Early career weights growth and manager; later career weights stability, comp, and flexibility.
  4. Weigh the criteria. Is growth more important than comp right now, and by how much? Relative comparisons are more accurate than pulling percentages out of the air.
  5. Score each offer 1-5 on each criterion using what you actually learned in the process — reference calls, the team conversation, Glassdoor / Blind with a grain of salt, the recruiter's answers to direct questions.
  6. Let the math rank them. Then challenge the winner: "If I take this and regret it in six months, what's the reason?" If you can answer that fast, address it before accepting.
  7. Use the deadline as a tool, not a whip. Most "exploding" offers can be extended a few days if you ask professionally. A company that won't give you 48 hours to make a life decision is showing you something.

Biases That Wreck Offer Decisions

Salary-number anchoring

The highest base becomes the reference point and every other factor is judged as a deviation from it. Fix this by modeling full comp for all offers first, so the comparison starts from real numbers, not headline ones.

Brand-name halo

A recognizable logo feels safer and more prestigious, and that glow bleeds into your scores for growth, team, and stability that you have no actual evidence for. Score what you observed in the process, not the brand.

Last-conversation glow

Whichever company you spoke to most recently, or had the warmest final call with, feels like the best. Recency is not signal. Re-score after a day's gap.

Sunk-cost of interview effort

You did six rounds for one company and one call for another, so accepting the six-round offer feels like "getting the return." The effort you already spent is gone regardless of which offer you take.

Counteroffer trap

Your current employer counters and the raise makes staying feel obvious. But the reasons you started interviewing usually aren't fixed by money, and counteroffer acceptances have a high re-departure rate within a year. Score the counter as its own option, honestly, on all criteria — not just comp.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I compare two job offers objectively?

List the criteria that matter to you (total comp, growth, manager, work-life balance, stability, location), assign each a weight based on importance, score every offer 1-5 on each criterion, then multiply and sum. The offer with the highest weighted total is your rational pick. The calculator above does this; a decision app like Decisio adds pairwise weighting and a devil's-advocate check.

Should I always take the higher salary?

No. Base salary is the factor that changes least over the next two years and says nothing about manager quality, growth, hours, or company stability — which are what determine whether you're still happy in month six. Take the higher salary only if the offers are close on everything else, or if your current financial situation makes near-term cash the dominant criterion.

How do I factor equity into the comparison?

Treat it as a separate criterion from base comp because the uncertainty is completely different. For public company stock, value it near face. For private companies, get the strike price, latest preferred price, share count, and vesting schedule, then model a wide range of outcomes including zero. Never let a large theoretical equity number anchor the whole decision.

What should I do about an exploding offer with a tight deadline?

Ask for an extension — professionally, once, with a specific new date. Most deadlines move a few days for a serious candidate. Use the time to do reference calls and score the offers properly. A company that refuses any extension for a life decision is giving you data about how it operates.
